# Introduction

In the past years, there has been a sharp, uncontrolled increase in the pigeon population in Singapore. The *Columba livia,* also known as the Rock Pigeon, is a non-native, invasive bird species that has colonised many parts of the world, including Singapore (NParks, 2025). These birds compete with the local wildlife, and their droppings are known to be carriers for other diseases and also cause severe property damage.

In Singapore, food waste from food establishments, like hawker centres, serves as a key food source for pigeons; they make food sources readily available for pigeons. Coupled with “Mercy Feeding”—feeding the pigeons as a religious/charitable act, for good “karma”—provides an easy food source for these birds (NParks, 2025).

Over time, this creates “hotspots”, where pigeons will gather in large numbers, knowing that their food will come to them as long as they are there. This leads to the persistent nuisance issue that some neighbourhoods are facing now. Neighbourhoods that are considered as “hotspots” are flooded with flocks of pigeons. After the feeding, not only food is leftover, but their droppings as well, which causes the problems that were mentioned above.

Given that pigeons nest and roost communally in crevices or ledges, high-rise buildings, commonly seen in Singapore, are ideal places for them to nest and roost. Singapore, as an urban city, also welcomes the stay of these birds (NParks, 2025).

The National Parks Board (NParks) and The National Environment Agency (NEA) have been putting in effort to defuse this issue for years. Yet, it still persists. Existing measures, such as enforcement, population control, food source management, and public education, had done little long-term success. This suggests that the current solution/approaches might have some flaws that hinders them from being fully effective; they are insufficient to address the root causes of this pigeon problem in Singapore.

In this report, we will analyse the problem and the stakeholders, evaluate the current solutions and their effectiveness, propose a solution and evaluate on its effectiveness, and lastly, map out our data collection plan and implementation timeline.

# The Problem

“The Pigeon Issue” has been around in Singapore for the past few years. Pigeons are seen crowding in certain “hotspots” in Singapore, especially in the HDB neighbourhoods. Another common sight would be people, especially the elderly, feeding the pigeons. These birds also thrive in hawker centers, where food is readily available to them, especially at the food-return stations. Over the years, their population growth has become a growing concern in Singapore. This issue can be understood through three main dimensions: environmental, economic, and social.

**Environmental Impact**

Pigeons are an invasive species in Singapore. They compete with native birds for necessities and resources such as food and nesting spaces. As pigeons nest in creaks, Singapore’s HDB buildings are perfect nesting spots for them. Their dominance in this urban city can reduce biodiversity. Over time, there will be an imbalance in the ecological dynamics of Singapore's biodiversity if this issue is not resolved promptly.

**Economic Impact**

In 2017, it was reported that the government has spent $800,000 on animal management measures, including costs that are spent on culling (Au-Yong, 2017). Senior Minister of State for National Development Desmond Lee was also unable to project if culling costs would increase, and urged the community to do its part to reduce animal-human conflicts (Au-Yong, 2017). This suggests that the pigeon issue can also contribute to the rising cost of government spending on animal management measures, if the overpopulation and feeding issues persist.

**Public Health Concerns**

Pigeon droppings can harbour harmful microorganisms, including bacteria and fungi. It dirties the walkways, facilities (e.g., playgrounds) and properties (e.g., houses, cars, windows, etc.). It becomes a nuisance and can also attract other pests, compounding sanitation issues.

While it is observed that pigeons can cause many problems to Singapore, the root cause is actually us, the humans. Pigeon feeding is a simple, yet strong driver to the problem. This makes food easily and readily available to the birds, supporting their population growth. Once the pigeons become accustomed to being fed at specific locations and times, they develop strong behavioural patterns that are difficult to disrupt, adding another weight to the problem.

# The Stakeholders

There are a few stakeholders that we need to first understand in order to tackle this issue.

**Government Agencies**

***NParks***

NParks is responsible for wildlife management and ecological balance in Singapore. They are Singapore's lead agency for greenery, biodiversity conservation, and wildlife and animal health, welfare and management (NParks, 2026).

In view of the current issue, NParks had urged citizens not to feed not just the pigeons, but any wildlife, so as to maintain the ecological balance in Singapore (NParks, 2025).

***NEA***

NEA oversees enforcement, sanitation, and public health. They ensure that the environment in Singapore is clean and sustainable, working closely with its partners and community to develop and spearhead environmental and public health initiatives and programmes (NEA, 2020).

NEA has worked with NParks, Singapore Food Agency (SFA) and the Town Councils to ensure that this issue is managed. Reducing food sources for the pigeons is one of the measures that they implemented (NEA, 2024). They enforced against table littering at hawker centres, food courts, and coffeeshops, and also worked together with the Town Councils, Hawkers’ Associations, stallholders, and cleaning contractors in proper ways to discard food, in hopes to solve the pigeon issue (NEA, 2024). However, they had also urged for the community’s support in these measures, and that “\[t\]he community’s support is critical to keep the population of such birds down.” (NEA, 2024).

**Residents**

Residents are directly affected by this issue. They face problems such as noise, droppings, and hygiene concerns. There are also mixed opinions towards stricter control measures, such as culling. While some are supportive of them, others may oppose as these actions are perceived by the residents as harmful to animals.

**Pigeon Feeders**

A significant portion of the pigeon feeders are the elderly individuals. They view feeding the pigeons as an act of compassion and kindness, or as religious merit. Their motives are deeply implicit, and are difficult to change. Even with stricter measures enforced against pigeon feeding, some are even willing to pay the fines multiple times just to feed these creatures.

These are some of the stakeholders whom we have identified that are either directly or indirectly part of the pigeon problem here in Singapore. These stakeholders need to be taken into account as we consider the proposed solution. With the government agencies, we need to first evaluate what has been implemented, so that our solution can come as an improvement of current solutions. Proposing a new product/system would also require such analysis and evaluation prior, in order for the solution to be effective.

# Current Solutions and Their Effectiveness

Despite years of enforcement, population control, and public education by NEA and NParks, pigeon feeding remains a persistent problem in Singapore. We will look at the current solutions across four categories and evaluate their effectiveness.

| Category | Solution | Effectiveness | Reasoning |
| :---- | :---- | :---- | :---- |
| **Enforcement** | Wildlife Act Fines | Low-Medium  | Fines of up to $10,000 exist, but feeding persists. Elderly feeders and emotional motivations limit deterrence |
|  | High Rise Littering (HRL) fines | Low-Medium | Approximately 27,100 HRL cases annually, despite fines. Penalties alone are insufficient.  |
|  | Camera Surveillance | Medium  | Approximately 30% detection rate. With around 3,300 enforcement actions between 2021 and 2023\. But it is limited by building design due to blind spots and offenders moving to other places. |
| **Population Management** | Trapping | Low-Medium  | Short-term relief. Pigeon populations replenish quickly as long as feeding continues. |
|  | Nest removal  | Low-Medium | Limited long-term impact unless paired with food source reduction  |
|  | Reducing perching surfaces | Medium  | Displaces pigeons from specific areas but does not reduce the overall population. They will just relocate.  |
| **Food Source Reduction**  | Hawker Centre Food and Water Management | Medium-High | Enclosed waste systems (e.g., Bukit Merah food centre) directly cut off the food supply. But coverage is not universal yet.  |
|  | Bird Feeding Enforcement | Low-Medium | Hampered by an elderly demographic, social sensitivity, and difficulty in monitoring all the hotspots continuously.  |
| **Public Education and Outreach** | Standees, Posters and Brochures | Low  | Widely distributed but generally ignored. It has a fine-focused messaging that has not shifted any behaviour |
|  | Anti-HRL Messaging  | Low | Social norm approach (“Be a Good Neighbour”), but it is passive |
|  | Eldering Outreach  | Medium | Interactive and targets the key demographic directly. But it has limited reach and is hard to measure effectiveness |

As seen, the current measures are not effectively changing mindsets, and repeat offenders continue to feed despite penalties. Outreach materials are passive and widely ignored, with limited social media presence, and current messaging lacks persuasion as it tells people what not to do rather than appealing to their values and emotions.

These solutions lack sustained effectiveness, and thus, the issue persists.

# Rationale and Approach

This would then lead us to our two initial approaches:

- Address the weak messaging brought across by current campaigns  
- Actively target the pigeons themselves 

The first option, while viable, requires a very long lead-time for the effects to be noticed, as it would take tremendous effort to change the mindset of the general populace. It might take years, or even decades, before feeding activities see a decline. And this might not be directly attributed to the campaigns themselves, seeing that most of the recalcitrant feeders are retirees.

Our team considered using targeted ads to reach the target audience, similar to what companies like META and TikTok do. However, there are also concerns with privacy and security, and does not seem ethically viable. THe next approach was an affective approach—using strong graphics and story-telling/framing to convince the public that the pigeons are the “bad guys”, and that feeding them would lead to detrimental effects. However, after evaluation, we took into account the public’s response and decided that it would not be too ethical and pleasing to the public to be targeting such threatful ads to them personally.

The second option presented its own set of challenges, but seemed more viable as current direct measures such as culling do appear to reduce the pigeon population, albeit at a rather cost inefficient and slow rate. 

The motivation stemmed from our site visits with the stakeholders (namely, NParks and NEA), where they voiced their grievances when it came to taking legal action against errant feeders. Despite countless tip-offs, many feeders have evaded being prosecuted due to the lack of proper video evidence that ties them to the feeding activities. Even when the offenders are prosecuted, they continue with their ways, as they believe it is good karma for them to continue doing so.

Moreover, some pigeon feeders have evaded capture by performing their feeding activities at the break of dawn, making it impossible for camera detection systems or officers to apprehend them. However, the stakeholders note that the pigeons have somewhat been conditioned to flock to a particular region during certain time periods, a result of scheduled feeding sessions over a long period of time. 

If we were unable to address the feeders, the next best option was to target the problem itself: the pigeons. Given that the pigeons have been conditioned to flock to areas during specific times for feeding, the inverse can be done to undo the programming that the errant feeders have been doing to them over the years. Thus, the idea to disrupt the pigeons’ normal roosting behaviour was theorised, taking a different approach from targeting the errant feeders through public messaging, which ironically requires a longer period of time to program.

# Proposed Solution

Our team identified pigeon feeding as the root cause of this problem in Singapore. Hence, propose an automated deterrence system that can help to reduce the roosting and gathering of pigeons in the current pigeon hot-spots, which can lead to a decrease in pigeon feeding.

This is an active solution that constantly disrupts the roosting patterns of pigeons. As a device perched on common roosting areas, such as grass patches, walkways, or even air-conditioning ledges, this portable device pumps strong jets of air towards the pigeons, so that they do not stay in that area for a long time. To combat the problem of pigeons roosting on this device itself, its design would be round, so that the pigeons will not be able to cling onto it.

The proposed deterrent system comprises two main elements:

- A camera  
- An active repellent system

The camera will be interfaced to a Single Board Computer (SBC) that controls the active repellent system. A simple image recognition algorithm runs locally on the SBC continuously scans a target area, and triggers the repellent system when a pigeon is detected in its field of view. The repellent system will be mounted on a simple turret system that enables it to rotate and turn as required, allowing it to concentrate its effects on a specific area as opposed to current market solutions, which can only fire in a general direction. 

This equipment has three parts: the camera, air pump, and ultrasonic speakers. When the camera detects pigeons in the area, it will activate the air jets, causing a strong jet of air to be blasted towards the direction of the pigeons. Simultaneously, the ultrasonic speakers activate, emitting a high-frequency sound inaudible to humans but audible to pigeons. The pigeons would then be deterred from this area. After some time, and after the population of pigeons in the hot-spot has reached its normal levels, this equipment can then be planted in another hot-spot till the issue is solved.

When the pigeons are unable to stay at the place for a prolonged period of time, it will naturally decrease the probability in that area. Having set this equipment up, it does not require constant in-person supervision, and the hardware is low-cost and can be easily installed and removed in various places at the same time. The idea is to disrupt their standard roosting behavioural patterns so that the pigeons will naturally be dissuaded from remaining in the area due to the active deterrence with the proposed system. 

In time, once the pigeons have been conditioned that the urban areas are no longer conducive to sustain their population, they would be inclined to migrate to other regions in Singapore, preferably less urban areas. By cutting them off from human contact, it should eventually break the cycle of the pigeons being reliant on humans feeding them, and eventually lead to a drop in the overall population.

In comparison to the current solutions, our proposed solution offers an active deterrence, it is scalable, and reduces reliance on human compliance.

# Testing and Prototyping

The development and prototyping process can be relatively quick due to our proposed framework of deploying on a Single Board Computer (SBC) like a Raspberry Pi. They are fairly easy to set up and can be used for development within a few hours. 

The prevalence of open-source data and guides would also speed up the development process, especially if we were to leverage popular Computer Vision (CV) models like YOLO that are pre-trained on general object recognition. This removes the need to train our own detection model and only requires us to fine-tune the model parameters to better suit our detection needs (pigeons). 

In addition, the overall script (should) be Python-based, when combined with good programming paradigms, should be relatively high-level and abstracted, making the debugging and development process fairly quick and simple. As an interpreted language, no compilation is required, and subtasks can even be executed live without needing to rebuild the whole project, making the development process much faster. 

Some inverse kinematics may be required for the operation and movement of the proposed servo turret. However, primarily the bulk of the development process will thus be dedicated to fine-tuning model parameters and making the model more efficient to run on a SBC like the Raspberry Pi. 

# Metrics to Test Success

The main objective of the prototype would be to successfully detect pigeons and have it trigger on pigeons and/or other unwanted avian species. Some potential pitfalls would be false positives and negatives, with the latter being more problematic, as pigeons could potentially adapt their behavioural pattern. Therefore, extensive testing on common pigeon hotspots will be required to determine the classification accuracy of the image detection algorithm. 

Thus, the following metrics have to be considered:

- Accuracy  
- Precision  
- Recall  
- Sensitivity

Initial testing will likely see low accuracy due to the limited data provided for training, and the model would need to be updated with training data obtained from deployment. Eventually, we expect the model to have higher accuracy with more data gathered from the deployment grounds. Deployment can also be done at multiple sites to better train the model. Issues of generalisation may arise, but with proper image processing, it is hoped that the model will retain a relatively high accuracy regardless of where it is deployed.
